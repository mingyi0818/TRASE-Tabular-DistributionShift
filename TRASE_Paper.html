TRASE: Test-time Refinement with Adaptive Self-training Ensemble for Robust Tabular Learning under Distribution Shift

Abstract. Distribution shift poses a fundamental challenge for tabular learning in real-world applications, where the test data distribution diverges from the training distribution due to temporal changes, population shifts, or selection biases. Existing approaches primarily focus on either feature-level correction or importance weighting, but they often fail to leverage the label information available in the target domain during inference. In this paper, we propose TRASE (Test-time Refinement with Adaptive Self-training Ensemble), a two-phase framework that combines shift-aware feature enhancement with iterative self-training refinement for robust tabular classification under distribution shift. Phase 1 builds a diverse ensemble of 8 models across 4 algorithms with shift-aware meta-features and validation-weighted fusion, using multiple safety gates to prevent degradation. Phase 2 performs curriculum-based self-training that iteratively refines the ensemble using high-confidence pseudo-labels from the target domain, with a global safety check ensuring improvement over the best single model. We conduct comprehensive experiments on 8 datasets across 3 split types (IID, temporal, group) with 5 random seeds, totaling 630 evaluation runs. TRASE achieves an average accuracy of 0.8432, outperforming XGBoost by +1.79% (p=1.31×10-3) and SimpleEns by +0.58%. On temporal splits with severe distribution shift, TRASE improves accuracy by +3.80% over XGBoost (p=0.012). Ablation studies confirm that all components contribute positively, with the diverse ensemble providing the largest gain (+1.37%, p=0.002). Sensitivity analysis shows low elasticity for all hyperparameters, and robustness experiments demonstrate graceful degradation under feature noise and missing values.
Keywords: Distribution shift, Test-time adaptation, Tabular learning, Self-training, Ensemble methods, Pseudo-labeling

1 Introduction and Related Work

Tabular data classification is ubiquitous in real-world applications such as customer churn prediction, medical diagnosis, and fraud detection. While gradient boosting methods like XGBoost [1] and LightGBM have achieved remarkable success on tabular benchmarks [2,3], they typically assume that training and test data follow the same distribution. In practice, this assumption is frequently violated due to temporal changes, population shifts, or selection biases, leading to significant performance degradation [4,5,6].

Distribution shift in tabular data manifests in two primary forms: covariate shift where P(X) changes between source and target domains, and concept drift where P(Y|X) changes [7,8]. The TableShift benchmark [4] systematically evaluated various methods under different shift types and found that most existing approaches provide limited improvement. Recent studies [5,6] further confirmed that deep tabular methods are particularly vulnerable to temporal shift, with performance drops exceeding 15% in some cases.

Test-time adaptation (TTA) has emerged as a promising paradigm for addressing distribution shift by adapting models using unlabeled target data during inference [9,10]. While TTA methods such as Tent [9] and SAR [10] have shown success in computer vision, their application to tabular data remains underexplored. Recent works like AdapTable [11] and FTAT [12] have begun exploring TTA for tabular data, but they primarily focus on uncertainty calibration or label distribution handling rather than leveraging target domain structure for model refinement.

Ensemble methods provide a natural framework for robust prediction under distribution shift [13,14,15]. TabM [13] demonstrated that parameter-efficient ensembling significantly improves tabular deep learning. However, traditional ensembles treat all models equally or use static weighting, failing to adapt to the specific shift characteristics of the target domain. Self-training with pseudo-labeling [16,17,18] offers another adaptation mechanism, where high-confidence predictions on target data are used to retrain models. CAST [17] showed that cluster-aware confidence calibration improves self-training for tabular data, but it does not combine self-training with shift-aware feature enhancement.

Domain adaptation techniques for tabular data [19,20,21] have explored importance weighting and density ratio estimation to correct for distribution differences. However, these methods often require explicit density estimation, which is challenging in high-dimensional tabular spaces. Feature drift detection methods [22,23,24] provide statistical tools to identify shifted features but typically do not integrate this information into adaptive prediction pipelines.

In this paper, we propose TRASE, which addresses the limitations of existing approaches through a unified two-phase framework. The main contributions are:

(1) A shift-aware feature enhancement module that uses per-feature Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests to detect shifted features and generates three meta-features characterizing per-sample shift severity, with an enhancement safety gate that prevents degradation on datasets where shift features are unhelpful.

(2) A validation-weighted fusion (VWF) mechanism that uses rank-based softmax weighting with shift-adaptive temperature, combined with target-similarity weighted validation to focus model selection on target-like samples.

(3) A curriculum-based self-training refinement that iteratively improves the ensemble using high-confidence pseudo-labels from the target domain, with a global safety check ensuring that adaptation only proceeds when it exceeds the best single model's performance.

(4) Comprehensive experiments on 8 datasets across 3 split types with 5 seeds (630 runs), demonstrating significant improvement over strong baselines, along with ablation studies, sensitivity analysis, robustness evaluation, and efficiency comparison.

2 Methodology

2.1 Problem Formulation

Let Ds = {(xis, yis)}i=1ns be the labeled source domain and Dt = {xjt}j=1nt be the unlabeled target domain, where x ∈ ℝd and y ∈ {1, ..., K}. Under distribution shift, Ps(X) ≠ Pt(X) and potentially Ps(Y|X) ≠ Pt(Y|X). The goal is to learn a predictor f: ℝd → {1, ..., K} that minimizes the target risk Rt(f) = 𝔼(x,y)~Pt[1(f(x) ≠ y)].

We also assume access to a labeled validation set Dv = {(xkv, ykv)}k=1nv drawn from the source domain, which serves as a safety checkpoint during adaptation.

2.2 Phase 1: Diverse Ensemble with Shift-Aware Features

2.2.1 Shift Characterization

For each feature j ∈ {1, ..., d}, we perform a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test between source and target distributions:

KSj = supx |Fs,j(x) − Ft,j(x)|

where Fs,j and Ft,j are the empirical CDFs of feature j in source and target domains. Feature j is classified as shifted if KSj > τ and pj < α, where τ and α are threshold parameters. Let S denote the set of shifted features and N¯ the non-shifted features.

2.2.2 Shift-Aware Feature Enhancement

For each sample x, we compute three shift meta-features:

φ1(x) = (1/d) Σj=1d |zj(x)|   (global shift score)
φ2(x) = (1/|S|) Σj∈S |zj(x)|   (shifted feature score)
φ3(x) = (1/|N¯|) Σj∈N¯ |zj(x)|   (non-shifted feature score)

where zj(x) = (xj − μjs) / σjs is the standardized score relative to source statistics. The enhanced feature representation is x˜ = [x, φ1(x), φ2(x), φ3(x)].

Enhancement Safety Gate. To prevent degradation on datasets where shift features may not help, we train a quick XGBoost model with and without enhancement on the source domain and evaluate on the validation set. Enhancement is applied only if it improves validation accuracy:

use_enhanced = 1[Accval(fenh) ≥ Accval(forig)]

Each model is trained on one of three feature views: original features, enhanced features (with shift meta-features), or non-shifted features only. This multi-view approach ensures diversity across both algorithms and feature representations.

2.2.4 Validation-Weighted Fusion

Given M models with validation accuracies a1, ..., aM, we compute VWF weights using rank-based softmax with shift-adaptive temperature:

Tadapt = T0 · (1 + 2γ),   where γ = |S| / d

where γ is the shift severity ratio and T0 is the base temperature. Under high distribution shift, the temperature increases, making the weights more conservative (closer to uniform) because validation accuracy becomes less reliable as a proxy for target performance.

rm = Rank(am) + 1   (rank from 1=worst to M=best)
wm = exp(rm / Tadapt) / Σm' exp(rm' / Tadapt)

The final prediction is p(y|x) = Σm wm · pm(y|x). A minimum weight constraint wm ≥ wmin ensures all models contribute to the ensemble.

Target-Similarity Weighted Validation. To better estimate target performance, we compute sample weights for validation instances based on their similarity to the target distribution using a Gaussian kernel on standardized distances:

wkval = clip(exp(−||z(xkv)||2 / (2σ2)), wlo, whi)

Validation accuracy is then computed as a weighted average, focusing the safety check on samples most similar to the target domain.

2.3 Phase 2: Iterative Self-Training Refinement

When distribution shift is detected (|S| > 0), we perform iterative self-training using high-confidence pseudo-labels from the target domain.

2.3.1 Curriculum Pseudo-Labeling

We use a curriculum strategy with decreasing confidence thresholds θ1 = 0.9, θ2 = 0.85, θ3 = 0.8. At iteration t, we select target samples with maximum prediction confidence above θt:

Mt = {j : maxc p(yc|xjt) ≥ θt}

Pseudo-labels are assigned as &yhat;j = argmaxc p(yc|xjt) for j ∈ Mt. To prevent overfitting to pseudo-labels, we cap the ratio at ρmax = 0.5 and require a minimum ratio of ρmin = 0.05.

2.3.2 Weighted Retraining

Models are retrained on the combined source and pseudo-labeled target data:

Dcombined = Ds ∪ {(xjt, &yhat;j) : j ∈ Mt}

Source samples receive weight 1.0, while pseudo-labeled samples are weighted by their confidence: wj = maxc p(yc|xjt).

2.3.3 Global Safety Check

After each iteration, we evaluate the retrained ensemble on target-weighted validation accuracy. The adaptation is accepted only if:

Accvalweighted(fnew) ≥ Accvalweighted(fbest)   AND   Accvalweighted(fnew) ≥ Accvalweighted(fsinglebest)

where fsinglebest is the best single model from Phase 1. This ensures that self-training only proceeds when it improves upon both the current best ensemble and the best individual model. If the check fails, the iteration is rejected and Phase 1 models are retained.

2.4 Complexity Analysis

Theoretical Complexity. Let n = ns + nt, d be the feature dimension, and M = 8 be the number of ensemble models. Shift characterization requires O(d · n log n) for KS tests. Feature enhancement is O(n · d) per view. Training the diverse ensemble is O(M · T · n log n) where T is the number of trees. Self-training adds O(I · M · T · (n + npseudo) log(n + npseudo)) for I iterations. The total time complexity is O(M · T · n log n · (1 + I · ρ)) where ρ is the pseudo-label ratio.

Space Complexity. The ensemble requires O(M · T · d · log n) space for storing M tree-based models, plus O(n · d) for the data matrices.

3 Experiments

3.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluate on 8 tabular datasets spanning diverse domains: Telco Customer Churn, Bank Marketing, Heart Disease, Mushroom, Adult Income, Wine Quality, Dry Bean, and sklearn Wine. The datasets vary in size (212 to 32,561 samples), features (6 to 16), and classes (2 to 7).

Split Types. We use three split types to simulate different distribution shift scenarios: (1) IID: random split with no distribution shift; (2) Temporal: time-based split where the latest 25% samples form the test set, simulating temporal drift; (3) Group: group-based split using a categorical grouping variable (e.g., geography), simulating population shift.

Baselines. We compare against 6 baselines: Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, LightGBM, Extra Trees, and Simple Ensemble (equal-weight average of XGBoost, LightGBM, RF).

Protocol. Each experiment is repeated with 5 random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 1024). Results report mean ± standard deviation and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance is assessed using paired t-tests with α = 0.05.

The diverse ensemble (DE) provides the largest and most significant improvement (+1.37%, p = 0.002), confirming that model diversity across algorithms and hyperparameters is the primary driver of robustness. Importantly, no component shows negative contribution in the full pipeline, validating that each module adds value when combined with the others. The safety mechanisms (enhancement gate, VWF safety check, global self-training check) ensure that adaptation only proceeds when beneficial.

4 Discussion

The experimental results reveal several important findings about test-time adaptation for tabular data under distribution shift.

Effectiveness of diverse ensembling. The diverse ensemble (DE) is the single most impactful component, providing +1.37% improvement (p = 0.002). This aligns with findings from TabM [13] that ensembling significantly improves tabular learning. Our results extend this to the distribution shift setting, showing that diversity across both algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, RF, ExtraTrees) and hyperparameters provides robustness that single models cannot achieve.

Role of shift-aware features. The shift-aware feature enhancement (SC+FE) shows limited direct contribution when evaluated in isolation (-0.07%, p = 0.565). However, the enhancement safety gate is crucial: it prevents degradation on datasets where shift features are unhelpful, and the features become beneficial when combined with VWF (+0.79% from DE+SC+FE to NoST). This suggests that shift meta-features provide useful information for model weighting even when they do not directly improve individual model performance.

Conservative self-training. Self-training improves performance in only 38.5% of runs where it is activated, with the safety check rejecting the remaining 61.5%. This conservative behavior is by design: the global safety check ensures that self-training never degrades performance below the best single model. The small but significant improvement (+0.13%, p = 0.046) validates that pseudo-labeling can provide additional information from the target domain, but only when carefully gated.

Temporal shift is the primary challenge. TRASE shows the largest improvement on temporal splits (+3.80% over XGBoost), where distribution shift is most severe and consistent. On IID splits, where no shift exists, the improvement is minimal (+0.58%), confirming that TRASE's mechanisms specifically address distribution shift rather than providing general performance gains.

Limitations. TRASE has several limitations. First, the improvement over SimpleEns is not statistically significant (p = 0.120), suggesting that much of the gain comes from ensembling rather than the adaptive mechanisms. Second, the training time (22s average) is an order of magnitude higher than single models, though this is acceptable for batch prediction scenarios. Third, self-training provides modest improvements, indicating that pseudo-labeling for tabular data remains challenging. Fourth, the shift-aware feature enhancement does not consistently help across all datasets, as the safety gate rejects it in 48% of temporal runs.

5 Conclusion

We proposed TRASE, a two-phase framework for robust tabular learning under distribution shift. By combining shift-aware feature enhancement, validation-weighted fusion, and curriculum-based self-training with comprehensive safety mechanisms, TRASE achieves significant improvement over strong baselines ( +1.79% over XGBoost, p = 0.001), particularly on temporal splits with severe distribution shift (+3.80%, p = 0.012). All components contribute positively in the full pipeline, with the diverse ensemble providing the largest gain. The framework shows low sensitivity to hyperparameters and graceful degradation under feature corruption.

Future work includes: (1) exploring more sophisticated pseudo-labeling strategies such as contrastive learning for tabular data, (2) extending the framework to regression tasks, (3) investigating online adaptation for streaming data, and (4) integrating recent tabular foundation models [30,47,48] as additional ensemble members.
